How supply chains became software

70 years of change, from the shipping container to AI, and what I expect next.

By Sachidananda Singh · 20 November 2026

Goods move slowly. Information about goods moves fast and arrives imperfect. Every supply chain runs on beliefs about its own state, such as records, forecasts and promises, and most of the technology built since the 1950s exists to keep those beliefs close to the physical truth.

This is how I read that history. I make the longer argument in my book, Supply Chains Are Distributed Systems.

1956. One box becomes the interface

In 1956 Malcom McLean loaded 58 metal containers onto a converted tanker in Newark. Fixing the size and fittings of one box separated every ship from every truck, crane and port. Cargo handling costs fell by an order of magnitude.

An engineer would call the container an interface standard. The network packet did the same job for data over a decade later.

1960s to 1980s. Planning moves onto computers

Material requirements planning put the bill of materials on a mainframe. It's the ancestor of the planning run that will execute in most large companies tonight.

Trading partners also began exchanging orders and ship notices electronically. The X12 standard for those documents was chartered in 1979, and it still carries a large share of high volume business orders. By the 1980s, Toyota's production system was teaching the rest of the world about pull signals and small batches.

1990s. Planning becomes packaged software

The first advanced planning systems sold forecasting and optimization as software a company could install. The deployments that paid off had clean data going in and guardrails between a forecast and a factory commitment. That lesson applies to every AI system being connected to a supply chain today.

2000s and 2010s. Demand moves at software speed

Online retail cut the wait customers expect from weeks to hours. Ships kept crossing oceans in about 3 weeks. The gap between how fast demand moves and how fast goods move is bridged by software that forecasts, positions stock and computes delivery promises, and that layer became where companies compete.

Cloud services arrived in the same years. Coordination machinery that the large internet companies had built for themselves could now be rented by the hour.

2020s. AI takes on routine decisions

After the disruptions of 2020 to 2022, companies started treating resilience as something to design and pay for. In the same period 3 capabilities matured. Machine learning forecasts hold up across millions of product and location pairs. Optimization runs continuously where it used to run overnight. Language models read the emails, PDFs and customs documents that earlier automation couldn't reach.

How well these work depends on the records underneath them, because an AI control layer amplifies whatever it's given. The order I recommend is honest state first, disciplined messages second and automation third.

What stays the same

Ships will still cross oceans in weeks. Lead time is still latency, and inventory is still the buffer that covers it. Customers will keep rewarding a kept promise over a fast one. The technology stack turns over and these constraints stay, so they're what I build on.

What I expect next

My most confident forecast is that manual document entry fades within the decade. It needs no new capability, only wider use of one that already works. Routine decisions such as replenishment and carrier assignment will move to supervised automation, with people setting policy and owning escalations.

Open commerce between software agents is the forecast I'm least sure of, because the rules for it don't exist yet. These are judgments. A reader in 2036 should score them.
